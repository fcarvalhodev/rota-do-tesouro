Is Brazil's debt a danger?

In the 12 months to August 2026, the consolidated public sector accrued R$1.18 trillion in nominal interest on its debt. Many people say Brazil will go bankrupt, that ordinary people pay for it, or that the most expensive thing is actually debt forgiveness for agribusiness. Here is what the numbers show, in simple words.

📊 See the summary in icons

The short answer

The debt is large and growing. Because most bonds are in reais, exposure to the exchange rate is lower, but that does not eliminate the risk of a crisis or a default. Creditors include residents and non-residents. Interest rates, growth, revenue and refinancing conditions all matter for sustainability.

What the debt is

When the government spends more than it collects, it borrows money. It does this by selling bonds, which are promises to pay the money back later, with interest.

It is like a family using a credit card. What matters is not only how much it owes, but in which currency it owes and how much it pays in interest.

The US owes more than US$40 trillion, more than everything the country produces in a year. But it owes in dollars, its own currency.

Countries that owe in dollars can run out of ways to pay

Argentina defaulted in 2001 and had to renegotiate US$65 billion in 2020. Today it is the IMF's largest debtor, and in 2025 it signed its 23rd agreement with the Fund.

Debt in your own currency reduces exchange-rate vulnerability

That does not make default impossible. Printing money has institutional limits and costs, and governments can also restructure domestic debt.

But it is not free

Creating too much money pushes prices up. And owing a lot makes lenders charge higher interest. The US feels this too: it spends about US$1.1 trillion a year on interest, more than on its armed forces.

The debt rose under Dilma, under Temer and under Bolsonaro during the pandemic. It fell in 2021 and 2022, with the economy's recovery and high inflation, which raises GDP in reais. Under Lula 3 it rose again, and most of the increase comes from interest.

R$1.18 trillion in interest

This is the total nominal interest accrued by the consolidated public sector in the 12 months to August 2026. It is not the same as cash payments in the federal budget; the indicator also includes swap results.

R$1.18 tn

That is almost 9 out of every 100 reais the whole country produced in the year.

Why are interest rates so high?

The Central Bank raises the Selic rate to hold down inflation. It stayed at 15% a year from June 2025 to March 2026, the highest in almost 20 years. In August 2026 it was at 14%.

More than half of the federal public debt was at a floating rate in August 2026. The Selic affects its cost, but the total bill also depends on other indexes, maturities and operations.

The government has almost no money left over to pay the interest. So it borrows more to pay, and the debt grows.

Defenders of the government say

The Selic is too high for today's inflation, and it is what makes the debt grow. Without interest, the 12-month budget gap was small: R$82 billion.

Critics of the government say

Interest rates are also high because the market distrusts the government's accounts. People who lend to big spenders charge more.

Who pays and who gets paid

Interest money comes out of everyone's taxes, or becomes new debt that someone will pay later. But it does not disappear: it goes to whoever lent. Out of every 100 reais of federal debt in reais, who the holders are:

“Pension” means the funds that pay supplementary retirement benefits to many workers. Investment funds include the money of people who invest through their bank. Part of the interest reaches the population through investments, funds and supplementary pensions. The distribution is unequal, and the indirect effects on credit, jobs and public services are not limited to those who hold bonds directly.

What costs more?

Many people say the most expensive things for the government are forgiving debts and helping agribusiness. The values below have different bases: actual 12-month interest of the consolidated public sector and federal tax benefits projected for 2026. They are not a ranking of all public spending:

The interest shown is almost double the cited projection for federal tax benefits, but the scopes and periods are not the same. Help for agribusiness weighs, but is much smaller. And the 2026 measure for rural debts was a renegotiation, with longer terms and interest, not forgiveness.

A tax benefit, or tax expenditure, is tax the government does not collect, such as the Simples Nacional regime for small businesses and the tax exemption on basic food items. It does not come out of the coffers, but it is money that does not come in. Even adding the states' benefits (R$334 bn), the total stays below interest.

Is it true?

Not shown

“Brazil will go bankrupt like Argentina.” The currency composition is different. That reduces one vulnerability, but it does not allow predicting a crisis nor guarantee zero risk.

Depends on the risk

“Debt in reais is less dangerous than debt in dollars.” It reduces the currency mismatch, but does not eliminate problems with interest, inflation and refinancing.

False

“So the debt is not a problem.” It costs very high interest and is growing. That money could go to other things.

True

“Interest alone came to R$1.18 trillion in 12 months.” That is the accrued nominal interest published by the Central Bank, not simply cash payments.

Half true

“Ordinary people pay the interest.” It comes out of taxes and new debt, which everyone pays. But part of the money goes back to people with investments, supplementary pensions or Tesouro Direto.

Limited comparison

“The biggest expense is agribusiness or debt forgiveness.” The values presented do not support that conclusion. Nominal interest, projected tax expenditures and rural renegotiation are different concepts with different scopes; nor do they prove that interest is the largest expense of the whole government.

True

“The United States owes more than Brazil.” In size and in proportion: their debt exceeds their entire GDP. The difference is that it is in dollars, their own currency.

False

“The debt only grows under Lula.” It rose under Dilma, under Temer and under Bolsonaro during the pandemic. It fell in 2021 and 2022, and rose again under Lula 3.

In short

  • Brazil's debt is almost all in reais. That reduces exposure to the exchange rate, but does not show there is no risk of default.
  • What weighs is interest: R$1.18 trillion in nominal interest accrued over 12 months, in the consolidated public sector.
  • The comparison with federal tax benefits is illustrative: it involves different scopes, periods and concepts.
  • The bill and its effects are distributed unequally. Creditors include banks, funds, investors and supplementary pension funds.
